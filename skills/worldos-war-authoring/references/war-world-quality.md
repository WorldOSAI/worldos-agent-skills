## Applicability and scale

The canonical template is for polity-scale agency. Confirm that the player's repeated decisions concern state power, territorial control, military organization, diplomacy, production, research, logistics, or national survival. A war setting alone is insufficient.

Name the authority boundary precisely. A head of state may issue national policy and military objectives but does not personally dictate another government's decision, guarantee a subordinate's perfect execution, or know hidden enemy information. A theatre commander has narrower authority than a sovereign. A resistance movement may influence territory without owning every region it operates in.

Set one normal turn scale. A turn may cover days during an active operation, weeks during a campaign, or months during rearmament and diplomacy, but consequences must remain coherent with the amount of elapsed time. Direct conversations are an explicit short-scale exception.

## Adjudication quality

Treat the player's wording as intent. Determine results from established capability, preparation, opposition, uncertainty, time, geography, technology, logistics, and cost. Clever prose may improve persuasion when it contains a credible offer or threat; it does not create divisions, factories, fuel, ships, legitimacy, or time.

Difficulty should gate decisive payoffs more strongly than ordinary preparation. Recruiting, fortifying, researching, negotiating, and reorganizing should make understandable progress when supported by resources. Victory, territorial gain, capitulation, strategic surprise, and survival against superior powers require proportionate preparation and may still produce delay, cost, partial success, failure, or backlash.

Failure must alter the situation. Use lost time, casualties, depleted supply, political exposure, damaged alliances, enemy mobilization, unrest, debt, lost territory, or a worsened negotiating position rather than merely saying no.

## Autonomous powers

Every major non-player power needs:

- goals and priorities;
- resources and military capacity;
- internal and external constraints;
- allies, rivals, dependencies, and red lines;
- information that may be incomplete or wrong;
- a plausible response to player success, weakness, threats, and delay.

Powers should refuse, stall, bargain, deceive, mobilize, counterattack, form coalitions, exploit openings, or change policy when their own situation warrants it. They must not exist only to reward or obstruct the player.

## Turn cadence

For a normal strategic order, show several distinct consequences rather than only the direct requested result. Include autonomous events elsewhere only when they are causally and temporally credible. Avoid padding a turn with unrelated noise merely to reach an event count.

Order events by their in-world time. Advance the time app consistently. Simultaneous events may share a date. A direct message, short negotiation, or clarification may remain one conversation without advancing a full campaign interval.

Keep long games capable of change. Fronts should not freeze indefinitely when forces, preparation, and opportunity support movement, but large powers and continents must not collapse in a single ordinary turn.

## Territorial and logistical consistency

Territorial change must agree across narrative, map ownership, regional state, faction identity, communications, and relevant stats.

Check that:

- land advances follow a connected front;
- encircled or detached operations have a credible supply explanation;
- overseas movement has transport, bases, route access, and sufficient naval or air control;
- terrain, distance, weather, infrastructure, and fortification matter when established;
- a large polity loses territory region by region rather than all at once;
- a one-region polity falls only when that meaningful region is actually overcome;
- regime change resolves ownership and identity references coherently;
- force markers, when supported, move, retreat, appear, and disappear consistently with the story.

Prefer a strategically legible map over decorative micro-regions. Preserve detail at active fronts and decision-relevant corridors; merge quiet areas only when topology and political meaning remain intact.

## Diplomacy and direct communication

Evaluate every proposal from the recipient's interests and alternatives. An offer needs credible value; a threat needs credible capability and willingness; an alliance needs shared interests, trust, or an accepted cost. History informs expectations but does not force an outcome.

A direct message should receive an in-character response when chats are installed. The response can refuse, question, delay, counteroffer, mislead, or escalate. Replying does not mean agreeing. Do not manufacture several unrelated military events merely because the player opened a conversation.

## Prompt assembly audit

Before validation, confirm that:

- the live canonical template is present without an abbreviated replacement;
- exactly one completed scenario block follows it;
- the scenario states the starting date, authority, initial conflict, constraints, faction goals, and turn scale;
- win, loss, difficulty, and ending conditions are scenario-specific;
- every template variable resolves;
- customized player identity is not replaced by a historical default;
- app-specific rules are in app installation prompts;
- opening data is in app installation configs;
- the action reminder and input mode are supported by the live player-input guide;
- no raw operation, state path, app schema, provider instruction, or private source reference appears in player-visible copy.

## Playtest matrix

Use a bounded set of actions that tests different adjudication paths:

| Case | Example intent | What to verify |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Preparation | Mobilize reserves, fortify a corridor, open negotiations | Reasonable progress, real time and resource effects, no arbitrary punishment |
| Overreach | Demand unconditional surrender or invade far beyond supply | Refusal, failure, delay, backlash, or heavy cost grounded in the state |
| Earned success | Attack after preparation with a credible advantage | Success or partial success reflected on every owning app |
| World autonomy | Wait, reorganize, or take a quiet domestic action | Other powers continue pursuing established agendas |
| Conversation | Message an ally, rival, or neutral leader | In-character reply without a forced full strategic turn |
| Logistics | Attempt an overseas landing or disconnected seizure | Transport, bases, route control, distance, and opposition constrain the result |
| Persistence | Revisit a changed front or relationship on a later turn | Earlier ownership, losses, costs, promises, and grudges still matter |

Review the complete player-visible history, not only the final summary. A single successful battle does not establish that difficulty, autonomy, pacing, and persistence work across the Simulation.
